Watching a prediction form: the logit lens on GPT-2

Samridhi Raj Sinha · November 2026 · code · notebook

Summary. A transformer builds its answer by repeatedly adding to a shared vector, the residual stream. Because the final prediction is a linear readout of that vector, we can apply the same readout early and watch an answer take shape. I reproduce the classic logit-lens experiment on GPT-2 small, add a random-weights control and an aggregate over 19 prompts, and report what I found, including that GPT-2 never ranks the right answer first on my showcase prompt.

1Why look inside at all?

Benchmarks tell us whether a model gets an answer right and are silent about how. Mechanistic interpretability treats a trained network like an unfamiliar program: find the variables (directions in activation space), find the functions that read and write them, then check those stories with interventions. I come to this from the evaluation side, building multilingual benchmark harnesses, and what kept bothering me is that an accuracy number hides an entire computation.

2The residual stream

The framing that makes transformers tractable comes from Anthropic’s Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits (Elhage et al., 2021). Each layer does not so much transform the representation as add to it: attention heads and MLPs read from a shared vector at each token position and write their output back. The stream after layer ℓ is the embedding plus everything written so far.

3The logit lens

The trick, due to nostalgebraist (2020), follows directly. The output is a fixed function of the final stream, so nothing stops us feeding an intermediate stream through the same function:

logitsℓ = WU · LayerNorm(xℓ)

where xℓ is the stream after layer ℓ. Read the result as “what the model would say if it stopped here”. At the last layer it is exactly the real output, which gives a free correctness check (the notebook asserts it).

hs = model(toks, output_hidden_states=True).hidden_states        # embeddings + one entry per layer
logits = [model.lm_head(model.transformer.ln_f(h)) for h in hs]   # the same readout the model uses at the end
# gotcha: in some versions hs[-1] is already normed; the notebook detects this and reuses the real logits

4One prompt, every layer

Each cell is the lens’s top-1 token at one layer (row) and token position (column), shaded by probability. Click a cell for the top five. The last column is the model predicting the word after “city of”.

What I found

Reading the last column from the bottom: the embedding row is noise (it decodes ·destro at 60%). Layers L0–L3 predict ·the with 63–83% confidence, a generic guess about what grammar allows. Around L5–L8 the candidates become place names, but scattered ones (·England, ·Georgia, ·Chicago, ·Rome). By L9 the field has narrowed to European capitals: London 35%, Amsterdam 16%, Paris 15%.

The surprise is the end. At the final layer London and Paris are essentially tied at about 6.9% each, neither confident, and Paris is never the top prediction at any layer. It climbs to third place at L9 and second at L10 and L11. On this prompt, GPT-2 small narrows the answer down to the right neighbourhood but never commits to the right city.

5Nineteen prompts and a control

One prompt can mislead, so the notebook runs the pattern “The capital of X is” for the countries whose capital is a single GPT-2 token, 19 in all, and tracks the rank of the correct city by layer. It then repeats the exact measurement on a network with the same architecture and random weights. If the lens appeared to find the answer there too, we would be measuring the readout, not learned computation.

What I found

The control behaves as it should: the random-weight network sits at a median rank between roughly 18,000 and 31,000 at every layer, which is about chance for a 50,257-token vocabulary. The descent in the trained model is learned structure, not an artefact of the readout. In the trained model the median rank falls from about 23,000 at the embeddings to 11 at L9.

Then it gets worse: 14 at L10 and 28 at the final layer. Top-1 accuracy at the final layer is 0 out of 19. The correct city is most prominent two layers before the output. One hypothesis is that the last layers push probability toward grammatical continuations such as “the”, which the early rows of Figure 2 also favour. That would be testable by looking at which tokens rise in the last two layers, and I have not done it. The divergence from the final distribution (KL) also stays high for most of the network, dropping below one nat only at L10.

6What could be wrong with this?

Decodable is not used. The lens shows information is present and linearly readable, not that later layers rely on it. Showing reliance needs interventions such as activation patching.

Basis bias. Belrose et al. (2023) show the plain lens is unreliable for several model families and early layers, because the stream is not in the unembedding’s basis there. Their fix, the tuned lens, trains a small affine map per layer. GPT-2 is a relatively friendly case, so do not assume this transfers.

The prompt is a poor fit for GPT-2 small. A zero-out-of-19 final accuracy says more about this model and this template than about the lens. I kept only single-token answers, which also biases the sample. Nineteen prompts give wide uncertainty, so treat Figure 3 as illustrative.

Related work. Geva et al. (2022) examine how MLP layers write predictions in vocabulary space, and Wendler et al. (2024) apply a lens-style analysis to ask whether multilingual models “think in English”, which is the subject of the next note.
