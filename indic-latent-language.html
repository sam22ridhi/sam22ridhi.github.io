Do multilingual models think in English? A Hindi logit lens

Samridhi Raj Sinha · November 2026 · code · notebook

Summary. I ask Qwen2.5-3B to translate a Hindi word into Chinese and use the logit lens to read its intermediate layers. The question, borrowed from Wendler et al. (2024) and moved to an Indian-language setting, is what the middle of the network “sounds like”. In my run, Latin-script tokens dominate the lens through most of the network, and the switch to Chinese happens abruptly, about 78% of the way through. That is suggestive of English-like intermediate states, but my test cannot separate English from other Latin-script tokens, and I explain why below.

1Why this matters for Indic NLP

Most strong open models are trained on text that is overwhelmingly English. If a model routes Hindi through something English-like internally, that shapes what we should expect: errors may be English-centric, Indic benchmark scores may partly measure translation, and “multilingual ability” could be thinner than it looks. At Lingo Labs I worked on EKA-EVAL, a framework covering more than fifty multilingual benchmarks, and a recurring frustration was that a score says nothing about what happens inside. This note is a first step toward that.

2Setup

The logit lens (note 01) reads an intermediate residual stream through the model’s own output layer. The prompt is a few-shot pattern, Hindi word → Chinese word, so the correct next token is a Chinese character; the input is Devanagari and English is neither. If English-like content shows up in between, it is not because the task asked for it. At every layer I sum the lens’s probability over all vocabulary tokens in three scripts, and I also track the probability of the correct Chinese token. Results average 11 test nouns after 4 few-shot examples.

P = lens_last(shots + f'हिन्दी: "{h}" - 中文: "')   # [layers+1, vocab] at the last position
mass = P @ mask.T                                       # mass on Devanagari / Latin / CJK tokens

3Result

What I found

Latin dominates the middle. From L1 through L27 Latin-script tokens carry roughly 18–83% of the lens mass, and above 45% in most of layers L14–L27. Chinese tokens stay under 15% of the mass until L27.

The hand-over to Chinese is abrupt and late. At L28 Chinese (44%) overtakes Latin (42%), and by L34 it holds 87% and by the output 96%. The correct Chinese character itself is below 0.1% until L27, 1.5% at L28, and reaches 58% only at the end. In this model, the decision to write Chinese seems to be made around layer 29 of 36.

Devanagari is almost invisible. Its mass is under 0.06% at every layer. I do not believe the model ignores a Hindi input; I suspect my classifier simply cannot see it (see below).

4What could be wrong with this?

Latin is not the same as English. Latin-script tokens include English words, but also code, names, transliterated Hindi and meaningless sub-word fragments. The measured mass is a proxy. The stronger test decodes against the specific English translation of each word, and the notebook now includes that measurement.

Early-layer Latin mass is probably an artefact. At L1 Latin mass is 83%. Early residual streams are far from the output basis, and decoding them through the output layer tends to yield junk sub-words that happen to be Latin. I would trust the mid-to-late layers much more than the early ones.

Tokenization hides Hindi. Qwen’s tokenizer splits Devanagari into many byte-level fragments, which a script classifier cannot label. The near-zero Devanagari curve is therefore uninformative about the input language.

Small and plain. Eleven concrete nouns, one model, one prompt format and a plain (untuned) lens, which is known to be less faithful in some models (Belrose et al., 2023). Presence of English-like content mid-network also does not show the model relies on it; that needs interventions.

5What I would do next

Track the probability of the English translation token specifically (already in the updated notebook), repeat with Hindi→Telugu or Tamil on a model that handles them, swap in a tuned lens, and run the reverse direction (English→Hindi) to see whether the same mid-network structure appears when Hindi is the output.
